📖Topics of the Day: #

  • Parts of the body
  • Punctuation and Intonation

🎯Learning Objectives: #

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  1. Identify and correctly use basic parts of the body vocabulary in spoken and written English, which would be useful in interpretation settings such as medical call centers.
  2. Recognize and apply basic punctuation marks (periods, commas, question marks, etc.) to improve clarity and meaning when reading aloud.
  3. Use appropriate intonation patterns (rising and falling tone) when reading sentences aloud to convey meaning, emotions, and sentence type (questions, statements, etc.).

🗒️Introduction: #

Today, you will learn how to name and describe different areas of the human body so you can communicate clearly in real-life situations, including professional environments like healthcare support or interpretation calls. You will also explore how punctuation marks help organize ideas in a text and guide you to read more clearly and naturally. Finally, you will practice using your voice effectively by applying the correct tone when reading, helping you express meaning, ask questions, and sound more natural in English.

🔑Key Concepts (Grammar Rules): #

📖 Tips to Help You Read Better in English:

  1. 🧠 Understand Before You Read Before reading aloud, take a moment to:
  • Look at the sentence
  • Identify key words
  • Think about the meaning

👉 If you understand the message, your voice will sound more natural.

  1. ✋ Use Punctuation to Guide Your Voice

Punctuation is like a “map” for your voice:

  • Comma (,): Make a short pause. 👉 “I went to the store, (pause) and I bought some milk.”
  • Period (.): Make a longer pause and stop. 👉 “She is my teacher. (stop) She is very kind.”
  • Question mark (?): Raise your voice at the end 👉 “Where are you going?” ⬆️
  • Exclamation mark (!): Show strong emotion (surprise, excitement, anger) 👉 “Watch out!” 😲

  1. 🎤 Use Intonation (Your Voice Tone) Intonation helps you sound natural: Yes/No questions: Rising tone ⬆️ 👉 “Are you ready?” Wh- questions (what, where, why, etc.): Falling tone ⬇️ 👉 “Where do you live?” Statements: Usually falling tone ⬇️ 👉 “I live in Guatemala.”

  2. 🔗 Connected Speech (Linking Words Together)

In English, words are often connected—not spoken separately. This helps you sound more fluent.

Consonant + Vowel Linking When a word ends in a consonant and the next word starts with a vowel, connect them. 👉 The consonant “moves” to the next word:

  • “Pick it up” → pi-ki-tup
  • “Turn off” → tur-noff
  • “Sit down” → (no linking here, both consonants)

✔️ This makes your speech smoother and faster. #

Consonant + Consonant Linking When a word ends with a consonant and the next word starts with the same consonant sound, we don’t pronounce it twice. 👉 Instead, we hold the sound a little longer and say it only once.

  • “Big girl” - bigirl (not big-girl)
  • “Hot tea” - hottea (long “t” sound)
  • “Good day” - gooday (one soft “d”)
  • “Stop please” - stoplease (one “p” sound)
  • “Class starts” - cla-sstarts (hold the s)
  • “Speaks Spanish” – speakSpanish (one “s” sound)

✔️ It should sound smooth, not like two separate words. #

📝 Summary: #

  • Punctuation guides your voice:
    • comma = short pause | period = stop | question = rising tone ⬆️
  • Intonation shows meaning:
    • 👉 questions go up ⬆️ | statements go down ⬇️
  • Connected speech = natural English:
    • 👉 link words together (no pauses)
    • 👉 same sounds (like s + s) = one longer sound
    • 👉 don’t add extra vowels (❌ eschool, ✔️ school)
